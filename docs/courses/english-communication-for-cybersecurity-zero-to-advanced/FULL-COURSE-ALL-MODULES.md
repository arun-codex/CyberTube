# English Communication for Cybersecurity — Zero to Advanced

> **Original video-first course blueprint and working draft — not an official certification or formally approved course release.**

**Audience:** Absolute beginners, aspiring SOC L1 analysts, blue-team interns, security researchers, ethical hackers, and cybersecurity workers who need clearer English.

**Scope:** English speaking, listening, pronunciation, reading, writing, reporting, interviewing and audience adaptation *in cybersecurity scenarios*. This is NOT a cybersecurity tools/hacking course.

**Structure:** 30 modules × 4 original lessons = **120 lessons**, six stages. Each module has a directly linked English teaching video, a supporting free English YouTube playlist, drills, role-play, assessment and free resources.

**Schedule:** 15–20 weeks at 6–9 hours/week for a beginner. This is an instructional estimate, not a promise of proficiency.

**Language:** English-only learning content; learner may use bilingual support for orientation. **Costs:** Linked videos can be watched for free; never promise third-party video availability or platform feature access.

## Study method (every module)

1. **Watch:** Open the primary YouTube English lesson; listen once for meaning, once with captions if needed and once while pausing to speak.
2. **Collect:** Save 5–10 useful words/phrases, their simple meanings and an original SOC sentence for each.
3. **Shadow:** Repeat 4–6 short English lines, focusing on intelligibility, pauses and stress; no accent imitation requirement.
4. **Apply:** Complete the original cybersecurity communication scenario, using only synthetic facts, without operating real hacking tools.
5. **Record:** Produce the specified speaking, reading or writing artifact; keep a dated portfolio.
6. **Reflect:** Check factual accuracy, audience, language clarity and next-step precision; improve and repeat.

## Baseline assessment (before Module 01)

- 60-second introduction: “Who am I and why do I want a cybersecurity role?”
- Listen to a free English workplace video and capture the main point, two details and an unanswered question.
- Write a 90-word simulated security status message using one test account and one test timestamp.
- Use the four-dimension rubric in `teacher/ASSESSMENT-AND-RUBRICS.md` and **retain the baseline** to compare against capstone performance.

## Final capstone

Complete Module 30 with a fictitious login-alert scenario: intake call, written ticket, escalation email, technical handover, a nontechnical business update, and a structured short interview answer. Rubrics value factual accuracy and communication, not hacking activity.

## Practice language — say it, record it, improve it
- “Please tell me if that term was unclear.”
- “The firewall blocked the connection.”
- “Based on the information currently available, I cannot confirm that conclusion.”
- “Could you confirm the next action and the person responsible?”

## Applied role-play and sample response
**Situation:** A teammate cannot understand the analyst saying “malware” and “firewall” during a simulated alert.
**Person A:** SOC analyst who must explain only the evidence available.
**Person B:** teammate, help-desk colleague, interviewer or manager who can ask for clarification.
1. Person A gives a 40–60 second explanation including who/what/when and the next action.
2. Person B asks “How do you know?” and “What is still uncertain?”
3. Person A distinguishes observation from interpretation and restates the requested action.
4. Swap roles and record one improvement.

**Example of appropriate wording (original):**
> “Please tell me if that term was unclear.” “The firewall blocked the connection.” We will verify remaining details before drawing a conclusion.

## Additional scenario card (original)

**Title:** Audio clarity  
**Given evidence:** A teammate mishears “firewall” as “file wall”.  
**Your communication assignment:** Read security terms at a natural pace, then break them into syllables if misunderstood.  
**Do not invent:** anything outside the stated evidence.  
**Check:** Exact word and a confirmed repeat.

**Practice variation:** Give the same information twice: once in simple English for a help-desk colleague, then once in a more structured form for the SOC team. Compare clarity, facts and next actions.

See [the complete role-play bank](../resources/30-SOC-COMMUNICATION-SCENARIO-CARDS.md) and [phrasebook](../resources/SOC-AND-ETHICAL-HACKER-ENGLISH-PHRASEBOOK.md).

**Success emphasis:** Intelligibility; accurate stress; repair when misunderstood. Also check that dates, counts, names and evidence are correct.

## Self-assessment (0–4 for each criterion; 16 possible points)
| Criterion | 0 | 2 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| English clarity | Unintelligible/absent | Mostly understandable; repair needed | Clear and concise for the audience |
| Factual accuracy | Fabricates or confuses facts | Most facts correct; omissions | Facts, uncertainties and evidence separated |
| Professional delivery | No audience/task awareness | Some tone/structure issues | Audience-appropriate and respectful |
| Actionability | No purpose/next step | Partly specified | Clear request/owner/next step |

**Progress rule:** This is formative evidence, not a quiz-unlock gate. Rewatch and resubmit as needed; no single score blocks course navigation.

## Safety, accessibility, and AI-use

- Respect different English accents: measure comprehensibility, not accent imitation. Offer captions, playback-speed options, transcripts when available, typed alternatives and untimed repeat attempts.
- AI may suggest language corrections during practice, but the final spoken demonstration should be the learner’s own recording or a separately documented accessibility-supported alternative. No unverified AI-generated security facts.

**Practice variation:** Give the same information twice: once in simple English for a help-desk colleague, then once in a more structured form for the SOC team. Compare clarity, facts and next actions.

## Safety, accessibility, and AI-use
- Use only synthetic hostnames, accounts and reserved test addresses such as `192.0.2.10` (documentation range). Never include real customer logs, credentials or private incidents.
- Respect different English accents: measure comprehensibility, not accent imitation. Offer captions, playback-speed options, transcripts when available, typed alternatives and untimed repeat attempts.
- AI may suggest language corrections during practice, but the final spoken demonstration should be the learner’s own recording or a separately documented accessibility-supported alternative. No unverified AI-generated security facts.
