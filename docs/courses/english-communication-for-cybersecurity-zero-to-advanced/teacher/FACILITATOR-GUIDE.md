# Facilitator Guide — Cybersecurity English Communication

## Audience and placement
A self-paced or facilitated English-for-SOC course for learners with little or no English. Technical cybersecurity examples are simulated and gradually become more detailed; the main learning construct remains English comprehension and production.

## Teaching cycle
1. **Preview vocabulary** for 5 minutes, using short original examples.
2. **Watch a linked English lesson** for 8–15 minutes (pause, replay, subtitles). Link rather than copy videos.
3. **Practice language form** for 8–10 minutes: sentences, question patterns, pronunciation or discourse markers.
4. **Role-play a fictional security workplace exchange** for 10–15 minutes: one analyst, one partner.
5. **Give feedback** for 5 minutes: identify one clear strength and two actionable improvements.
6. **Save evidence** and reflect; allow repeated attempts without punitive unlocking.

## Teaching principles
- English communication is the core: avoid turning the course into a technical SOC lab or hacking tutorial.
- Teach “We observed …” versus “We confirmed …” versus “We suspect …” and prohibit converting suspicion into fact.
- Preserve exact times, units, counts and asset names; use reserved test identifiers such as `192.0.2.10` or `example.com`.
- Do not evaluate accent conformity. Use intelligibility and successful communication repair as primary speech criteria.
- Provide text substitutes, captioning and extra time where needed. Speaking outcomes need a valid equivalent assessment plan if a disability affects recording or oral participation.
- Keep students’ personal or company incidents, credentials, customer data, and real usernames out of all tools and assignments.
- Allow AI tools only for *practice feedback* and citation/source checks; do not credit AI-generated scripts as independent learner English performance.

## Instructor model feedback examples
- **Clarity:** “Your opening states what happened. Shorten the next sentence and place the event time before the investigation steps.”
- **Factuality:** “You wrote ‘the attacker logged in’ but your notes show only failed attempts. Change it to ‘we observed repeated failed logins; the cause is unconfirmed.’”
- **Action:** “End with who should respond and what confirmation is needed.”
- **Pronunciation:** “Please speak the letters and numbers of the hostname more slowly; pronunciation variety is fine.”

## 18-week suggested timetable
- Weeks 1–3: Modules 1–5: basic sounds, identifiers, introductions, sentences, questions.
- Weeks 4–6: Modules 6–10: tense, listening, speech repair, plain-language security terms.
- Weeks 7–9: Modules 11–15: describing systems, clarifications, tickets, notes and email.
- Weeks 10–12: Modules 16–21: chat, calls, shift handover, timelines, triage, phishing.
- Weeks 13–15: Modules 22–26: risk, intelligence, executive writing, meetings and disagreement.
- Weeks 16–18: Modules 27–30: presentations, pressure updates, interview and capstone.

## Review and QA roles (not assigned)

- English teaching reviewer: check CEFR progression, video instructional fit, clarity and rubrics.
- SOC/incident-response reviewer: check technical truth, simulated scenarios and evidence language.
- Accessibility reviewer: check caption availability and equivalent access to each language task.
