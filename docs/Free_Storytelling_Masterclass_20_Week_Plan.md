# 1. Your learning target

By the end of 20 weeks, you should be able to:

- Turn an observation, memory, question or idea into a story premise.
- Build believable characters with goals, needs, contradictions and arcs.
- Structure a story so that events cause one another rather than merely happen in sequence.
- Create conflict, tension, suspense, surprise and emotional movement.
- Use dialogue, setting, point of view, theme, subtext and imagery deliberately.
- Tell a true personal story clearly and ethically.
- Deliver a story aloud with effective pacing, voice, pauses and body language.
- Use images, shots, sound and editing to tell a story in video form.
- Use storytelling to explain an idea or make a persuasive presentation without distorting facts.
- Analyse other stories, receive feedback, revise your work and explain why your creative choices work.

## What “all-round storyteller” means in this course

You will train four connected forms:

| Form | What you practise | Example output |
|---|---|---|
| Written storytelling | Fiction, nonfiction, scenes, dialogue and narrative structure | Short story or script |
| Oral storytelling | Personal stories, public speaking, voice and audience connection | 3–5 minute live story |
| Visual storytelling | Images, framing, storyboards, sequence and editing | 2–3 minute video |
| Purposeful storytelling | Explaining, teaching, presenting and persuading | 3–5 minute presentation |

The course has one shared foundation, but these forms are not identical. A strong written story may not work when spoken aloud; a good video may need fewer words and more visual information. You will learn to adapt rather than copy one format into another.

# 2. Weekly time plan: exactly five hours

Study for **five sessions of 60 minutes** each week. Choose any five days that fit your schedule. Keep the same rhythm when possible.

| Session | Time | What to do |
|---|---:|---|
| Session 1 — Learn | 60 min | Study the week's concept and watch/read the assigned resource. Take brief notes. |
| Session 2 — Analyse | 60 min | Analyse a film scene, short story, speech, advertisement or true story using the week's concept. |
| Session 3 — Create | 60 min | Complete the main writing or story-design exercise. Do not edit every sentence while drafting. |
| Session 4 — Perform/adapt | 60 min | Tell the story aloud, translate/adapt it between English and Hindi, or create a visual version. |
| Session 5 — Review | 60 min | Revise, seek feedback, score the work and add it to your portfolio. |

### Suggested breakdown inside each one-hour session

- 5 minutes: Recall what you learned last time.
- 20 minutes: Watch, read or study one concept.
- 10 minutes: Write notes in your own words.
- 20 minutes: Practise the skill.
- 5 minutes: Record what worked and what to improve.

For a creation or performance session, use most of the hour to make something. Do not force the same timing if the assignment needs more practice; keep the **weekly total** near five hours.

## Minimum weekly deliverables

Every week, save at least:

1. One page of concept notes.
2. One story analysis.
3. One original exercise or draft.
4. One recording or adaptation exercise when relevant.
5. One self-review with a score and next improvement.

# 3. Bilingual learning system: English + Hindi

Use English as the main source language for many of the linked courses, but do not let English vocabulary slow down your thinking. Learn the concept in English, explain it simply in Hindi, and practise expressing it in both languages.

## The bilingual workflow

For each new concept:

1. **Learn it in English.** Watch the source lesson with English subtitles where available.
2. **Explain it in Hindi.** Write a two- or three-line explanation in the Hindi you naturally use.
3. **Make one example.** Use an Indian setting, relationship, family situation, college, workplace, neighbourhood or familiar everyday experience.
4. **Create in your strongest language first.** A first draft should prioritise the story, not translation accuracy.
5. **Adapt it into the other language.** Preserve intent, emotion, character voice and cultural context. Do not translate word-for-word if the result sounds unnatural.
6. **Speak both versions aloud.** Notice where the rhythm, humour, idioms or emotional emphasis changes.

## Bilingual quality rule

Do not assume that English is automatically more professional or Hindi is automatically more emotional. Both can do both. Choose the language that best serves the intended audience and the character's voice. You may naturally use code-switching when it fits a real speaker, but avoid adding English words only to sound sophisticated.

# 5. The 20-week curriculum: 100 lessons

Every week contains five one-hour lessons. The listed resources are study inputs; the assignments are the actual training. If you miss a session, continue from the point you stopped rather than doubling the workload and burning out.

## Level 1 — Foundations (Weeks 1–4)

### Week 1 — What is a story?

**Goal:** Understand the difference between a story, a list of events, an explanation and a summary.

**Learn:** Character, change, audience, purpose, beginning–middle–end, curiosity and emotional connection.

**Resources:** [TED-Ed: 4 Ways to Tell a Great Story](https://ed.ted.com/lessons/4-ways-to-tell-a-great-story); begin [Pixar in a Box](https://www.khanacademy.org/partner-content/pixar/storytelling).

1. **Lesson 1 — Story versus information:** Watch the TED-Ed lesson. Write your own definition of a story in English and Hindi. Give one example of a story and one example of plain information.
2. **Lesson 2 — The audience:** Choose a familiar story and identify who it is for, what the audience knows and what the audience wants to know next.
3. **Lesson 3 — Change:** Write three tiny narratives: a person wants something, something changes, and the result is different from the start.
4. **Lesson 4 — Tell a real moment:** Tell a real event from your life in 60 seconds. Record it on your phone. Do not worry about performance yet.
5. **Lesson 5 — First analysis:** Analyse a short film scene, story or personal anecdote. Mark its opening, important change and ending. Write three strengths and one weakness.

**Weekly assignment:** A 150–250 word story about one ordinary event, plus a 60-second spoken version. Keep both English and Hindi versions if possible.

**Deliverable:** `W01_first_story_draft` and `W01_first_story_audio`.

### Week 2 — Observation, memory and idea generation

**Goal:** Find stories in everyday life and convert raw ideas into useful premises.

**Learn:** Observation, sensory detail, memory, curiosity, “What if?” questions, premise and originality.

**Resources:** Pixar's “We are all storytellers” / first section; [Andrew Stanton's TED talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KxDwieKpawg).

1. **Lesson 1 — Collect observations:** Record ten details from a real place: sounds, objects, behaviour, mood and small contradictions.
2. **Lesson 2 — Memory as material:** Write one memory using concrete details. Separate what you remember clearly from what you are guessing.
3. **Lesson 3 — What if?:** Turn five ordinary observations into “What if?” story ideas. Make the situation unusual but understandable.
4. **Lesson 4 — Premise practice:** Reduce three ideas to one sentence each: main character + situation + central problem.
5. **Lesson 5 — Select the strongest idea:** Score each idea for clarity, conflict, emotional potential and your own interest. Choose one to develop.

**Weekly assignment:** Create an idea bank of 20 premises. Select one idea and write a 100-word pitch in English and Hindi.

**Deliverable:** `W02_idea_bank` and `W02_premise_pitch`.

### Week 3 — Story spine, hook and promise

**Goal:** Build a simple story that has direction and makes the audience want to continue.

**Learn:** Hook, setup, inciting incident, story spine, question raised by the opening and promise to the audience.

**Resources:** Pixar story-spine activities; Andrew Stanton TED talk.

1. **Lesson 1 — Story spine:** Use this starter structure: Once upon a time… Every day… Until one day… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then…
2. **Lesson 2 — Hooks:** Write five different openings for the same story: a surprising fact, a decision, a question, an image and a moment of action.
3. **Lesson 3 — The story promise:** Decide what type of experience the audience expects: funny, suspenseful, moving, informative, frightening or inspiring.
4. **Lesson 4 — Causality:** Replace “and then” chains with “because”, “but” and “therefore”. Check whether each major event causes or changes the next one.
5. **Lesson 5 — Pitch aloud:** Explain the story in 30 seconds. Ask a listener what they think the story is about and what they want to know.

**Weekly assignment:** Create a one-page story outline with a hook, setup, central problem, three major events and ending.

**Deliverable:** `W03_story_spine` and `W03_30_second_pitch`.

### Week 4 — Plot and three-act structure

**Goal:** Understand how a story develops through setup, confrontation and resolution.

**Learn:** Act I, Act II, Act III, inciting incident, plot point, midpoint, climax, resolution and pacing.

**Resources:** [StudioBinder: Three-Act Structure Explained](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvqjp1CxxD8); Pixar story-structure lesson.

1. **Lesson 1 — Three acts:** Learn the job of setup, confrontation and resolution. Do not treat the acts as fixed percentages.
2. **Lesson 2 — Turning points:** Identify the event that makes the story's original situation impossible to continue.
3. **Lesson 3 — Midpoint and escalation:** Give the protagonist a new understanding, setback or change of plan halfway through.
4. **Lesson 4 — Climax and resolution:** Design a final choice or confrontation that tests the central goal and character.
5. **Lesson 5 — Reverse outline:** Analyse a favourite film or short story. Write its major beats without retelling every scene.

**Weekly assignment:** Write a 10-beat outline for an original story. Explain what changes at each beat.

**Deliverable:** `W04_three_act_outline`.

## Level 2 — Character and narrative development (Weeks 5–8)

### Week 5 — Creating believable characters

**Goal:** Make characters specific, memorable and capable of driving a story.

**Learn:** Characterisation, internal and external traits, contradiction, backstory, behaviour and voice.

**Resources:** [OpenLearn: Start Writing Fiction](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/history-the-arts/creative-writing/start-writing-fiction); Pixar character lesson.

1. **Lesson 1 — Specificity:** Build one character using five concrete details: habit, object, skill, fear and contradiction.
2. **Lesson 2 — Internal versus external:** Separate appearance and public behaviour from beliefs, private motives and emotional patterns.
3. **Lesson 3 — Backstory:** Write only the past details that influence the character's present choices. Avoid biography that does not affect the plot.
4. **Lesson 4 — Character voice:** Write the same sentence as three different people. Change word choice, rhythm, confidence and what each person avoids saying.
5. **Lesson 5 — Character interview:** Answer ten questions as the character. Then write a short scene where the character makes a decision.

**Weekly assignment:** Create three character profiles, each with a contradiction, fear, skill, weakness and distinctive voice.

**Deliverable:** `W05_character_profiles`.

### Week 6 — Goals, needs, obstacles, stakes and arcs

**Goal:** Understand why a character acts and how the story changes them.

**Learn:** External goal, internal need, motivation, obstacle, stakes, choice, consequence and character arc.

**Resources:** Pixar character lesson, especially wants versus needs, obstacles, character arc and stakes.

1. **Lesson 1 — Want versus need:** Identify what a character consciously wants and what they may need to understand or change.
2. **Lesson 2 — Motivation:** Give the goal a believable reason. Ask why it matters now.
3. **Lesson 3 — Obstacles:** Create three increasingly difficult obstacles: practical, social and internal.
4. **Lesson 4 — Stakes:** Define what the character can lose, what others can lose and what happens if they do nothing.
5. **Lesson 5 — Arc:** Describe the character's starting belief, the pressure that challenges it and the final choice that reveals change.

**Weekly assignment:** Build one character arc and write a 500-word scene in which a decision exposes the character's flaw or strength.

**Deliverable:** `W06_character_arc` and `W06_scene`.

### Week 7 — Point of view, narration and dialogue

**Goal:** Control who tells the story, what they know and how people speak.

**Learn:** First person, second person, third-person limited, omniscient narration, unreliable narrator, dialogue, subtext and exposition.

**Resources:** OpenLearn fiction course; Pixar story lessons; analyse dialogue in a favourite film scene.

1. **Lesson 1 — Point of view:** Retell one event from first person and third person. Notice what information changes.
2. **Lesson 2 — Narrator knowledge:** Decide what the narrator knows, misunderstands, hides or cannot know.
3. **Lesson 3 — Dialogue:** Write a conversation where each speaker wants something different.
4. **Lesson 4 — Subtext:** Write a scene where the characters discuss an ordinary subject while avoiding the real issue.
5. **Lesson 5 — Exposition edit:** Remove dialogue that only explains information both characters already know. Replace it with conflict, action or implication.

**Weekly assignment:** Write a 600–800 word scene with two characters, a clear point of view, a goal for each speaker and subtext.

**Deliverable:** `W07_dialogue_scene`.

### Week 8 — Setting, worldbuilding and genre

**Goal:** Make the story world feel real and make the setting influence events.

**Learn:** Setting, atmosphere, sensory details, world rules, genre expectations, research and meaningful objects.

**Resources:** OpenLearn fiction material on setting and genre; Pixar visual-language lesson.

1. **Lesson 1 — Setting as pressure:** Choose a place that makes the character's goal easier or harder.
2. **Lesson 2 — Sensory detail:** Describe a place through sound, temperature, texture, smell and light without listing every sense mechanically.
3. **Lesson 3 — World rules:** Define three rules of the story world. Show how breaking one rule causes a problem.
4. **Lesson 4 — Genre:** Retell the same premise as a mystery, comedy and drama. Note how audience expectations change.
5. **Lesson 5 — Research and authenticity:** Identify what needs factual research. Mark uncertain details and avoid pretending to know what you have not verified.

**Weekly assignment:** Write an 800–1,000 word short scene in a vivid setting. Make the setting affect the plot rather than serve as decoration.

**Deliverable:** `W08_setting_scene`.

## Level 3 — Emotion, tension and narrative control (Weeks 9–12)

### Week 9 — Conflict, tension and suspense

**Goal:** Keep an audience interested by making the outcome matter and delaying certainty for a reason.

**Learn:** Conflict types, scene goal, opposition, escalation, suspense, mystery, stakes and reversals.

**Resources:** Pixar structure lesson; Lessons from the Screenplay channel: https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCErSSa3CaP_GJxmFpdjG9Jw

1. **Lesson 1 — Conflict types:** Build an external conflict, interpersonal conflict and internal conflict for one protagonist.
2. **Lesson 2 — Scene objective:** Give every scene a character who wants something and an obstacle that prevents an easy result.
3. **Lesson 3 — Escalation:** Make each attempt change the situation. Do not repeat the same obstacle at the same intensity.
4. **Lesson 4 — Suspense versus surprise:** Write one scene where the audience knows danger is coming and one where the audience learns it at the same time as the character.
5. **Lesson 5 — Scene test:** Mark where tension rises, falls or disappears. Remove or rewrite a scene that changes nothing.

**Weekly assignment:** Write a 700–900 word suspense scene with a clear goal, escalating obstacles and a consequential ending.

**Deliverable:** `W09_suspense_scene`.

### Week 10 — Emotion, pacing and audience engagement

**Goal:** Create emotional movement without forcing the audience to feel something.

**Learn:** Empathy, identification, emotional contrast, pacing, beats, silence, release, catharsis and restraint.

**Resources:** Andrew Stanton TED talk; TED-Ed storytelling lesson; The Moth story examples.

1. **Lesson 1 — Emotional investment:** Identify what the audience needs to understand about a character before a difficult moment matters.
2. **Lesson 2 — Show versus tell:** Rewrite abstract emotion as behaviour, choice, sensory detail or dialogue.
3. **Lesson 3 — Pacing:** Shorten a slow passage and expand a key moment. Compare how the timing changes the experience.
4. **Lesson 4 — Contrast:** Place calm before tension, humour before seriousness or relief before a new problem, only when it supports the story.
5. **Lesson 5 — Emotional audit:** Ask what emotion each major beat is intended to create and whether the scene earns it.

**Weekly assignment:** Rewrite a previous story to improve emotional clarity and pacing. Keep the original draft so you can compare.

**Deliverable:** `W10_emotional_revision` and a short revision note.

### Week 11 — Theme, symbolism, metaphor and subtext

**Goal:** Give a story meaning beyond the sequence of events.

**Learn:** Theme, central question, motif, symbol, metaphor, image patterns, subtext and ambiguity.

**Resources:** Pixar theme lesson; OpenLearn Creative Writing and Critical Reading (preview selected material).

1. **Lesson 1 — Theme as a question:** Turn a broad topic such as trust, ambition or belonging into a question a character must face.
2. **Lesson 2 — Theme through choice:** Make the protagonist's decision reveal the story's central question. Avoid adding a speech that explains the moral.
3. **Lesson 3 — Motif:** Select an object, image, sound or repeated action that changes meaning as the story progresses.
4. **Lesson 4 — Metaphor:** Use one meaningful comparison to clarify an emotional or abstract experience. Remove decorative metaphors that confuse the scene.
5. **Lesson 5 — Subtext analysis:** Analyse a scene where characters do not say exactly what they mean. Write what is spoken and what is implied.

**Weekly assignment:** Write a 500–700 word story with a clear central question and one recurring image or object. Do not state the theme directly in the final paragraph.

**Deliverable:** `W11_theme_and_motif_story`.

### Week 12 — Foreshadowing, twists and multiple timelines

**Goal:** Surprise the audience fairly and control the order in which information is revealed.

**Learn:** Foreshadowing, clues, red herrings, payoff, twist, reveal, flashback, flashforward and nonlinear sequence.

**Resources:** Pixar pitching/feedback and storyreel lessons; analyse a mystery or thriller you already know.

1. **Lesson 1 — Foreshadowing:** Plant one clue early that becomes meaningful later. It should be visible in hindsight but not announce the answer.
2. **Lesson 2 — Payoff:** Connect a detail introduced earlier to a later action, decision or revelation.
3. **Lesson 3 — Fair twists:** Create a twist that changes interpretation without contradicting established facts.
4. **Lesson 4 — Timeline:** Write a short story in chronological order, then rearrange it using a flashback or delayed reveal. Compare both versions.
5. **Lesson 5 — Reader fairness:** Check whether the narrator hid information unfairly, whether clues were available and whether the ending feels earned.

**Weekly assignment:** Write a 700–1,000 word short mystery or reveal-based story with at least two clues and a logical payoff.

**Deliverable:** `W12_twist_story` plus a clue/payoff map.

## Level 4 — Real-life, oral, visual and persuasive formats (Weeks 13–16)

### Week 13 — Personal and true storytelling

**Goal:** Turn real experiences into a compelling story while preserving honesty and respecting other people's privacy.

**Learn:** Personal narrative, life map, scene, stakes, vulnerability, memory, truth, consent and narrative boundaries.

**Resources:** [Acumen Storytelling for Change](https://acumenacademy.org/course/storytelling-change); [The Moth storytelling guidelines](https://www.themoth.org/tell-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks); [The Moth stories](https://www.themoth.org/Stories).

1. **Lesson 1 — Life map:** List meaningful moments, decisions, failures, discoveries and changes. Choose a story that is yours to tell.
2. **Lesson 2 — The central moment:** Select one moment rather than summarising your entire life. Identify what you wanted and what was at risk.
3. **Lesson 3 — Scene and reflection:** Rebuild the event with concrete details, then add only the reflection needed to help the audience understand its meaning.
4. **Lesson 4 — Truth and boundaries:** Separate memory from certainty. Change or omit identifying details when appropriate. Do not fabricate dialogue or events and present them as fact.
5. **Lesson 5 — First-person performance:** Tell the story in three minutes. Ask a listener what moment stayed with them and what they did not understand.

**Weekly assignment:** Write and record a true three-minute personal story. Create one English and one Hindi version, adapting naturally.

**Deliverable:** `W13_personal_story_script` and two recordings.

### Week 14 — Oral storytelling and delivery

**Goal:** Tell a clear and engaging story without depending on a written script.

**Learn:** Vocal variety, pace, pauses, articulation, emphasis, gestures, posture, eye contact, audience connection and timing.

**Resources:** OpenLearn [Talk the Talk](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/education-development/talk-the-talk); Acumen Storytelling for Change; TED-Ed Public Speaking 101 collection.

1. **Lesson 1 — Voice baseline:** Record a one-minute story. Listen for pace, clarity, filler words, volume and sentence endings.
2. **Lesson 2 — Vocal choices:** Repeat the same paragraph with three different intentions. Use pauses and emphasis, not theatrical shouting.
3. **Lesson 3 — Physical delivery:** Practise standing or sitting naturally. Use gestures to clarify action, not to decorate every sentence.
4. **Lesson 4 — Memory structure:** Reduce your script to five cue words or beats. Tell the story from the beats instead of memorising every line.
5. **Lesson 5 — Audience test:** Deliver a three-minute version to a person or camera. Ask them to summarise the story and identify its turning point.

**Weekly assignment:** Deliver a three-to-five-minute personal story without reading. Keep the recording and a self-review.

**Deliverable:** `W14_oral_story_video_or_audio`.

### Week 15 — Visual and video storytelling

**Goal:** Understand how images, framing, order, sound and editing communicate story information.

**Learn:** Visual language, shot size, angle, composition, action, sequence, storyboard, visual continuity, sound and edit rhythm.

**Resources:** Pixar visual-language and film-grammar lessons; [StudioBinder](https://www.youtube.com/@StudioBinder); Pixar storyboard and storyreel activities.

1. **Lesson 1 — Show without words:** Describe a character's emotion using only action and visual detail. Do not use explanatory narration.
2. **Lesson 2 — Shot sizes:** Learn wide, medium and close-up shots. Decide what information each shot gives the audience.
3. **Lesson 3 — Storyboard:** Draw or write six frames showing a character's goal, obstacle and change. Simple stick figures are enough.
4. **Lesson 4 — Edit and sound:** Record a sequence on your phone. Notice how order, pause, ambient sound and cut length change meaning.
5. **Lesson 5 — Rough cut review:** Make a 30–60 second visual story. Show it without explaining it and ask what the viewer understood.

**Weekly assignment:** Create a 60–90 second video with a beginning, change and ending. Use original footage or assets you have permission to use.

**Deliverable:** `W15_visual_storyboard` and `W15_video_v1`.

### Week 16 — Explanatory, business and persuasive storytelling

**Goal:** Use narrative to make an idea understandable and memorable without manipulating the audience or weakening the evidence.

**Learn:** Audience, purpose, problem, insight, evidence, example, benefit, call to action, analogy and narrative framing.

**Resources:** TED-Ed Public Speaking 101; OpenLearn Talk the Talk; Acumen Storytelling for Change.

1. **Lesson 1 — Audience first:** Identify what the audience already knows, what they need and what decision or understanding you want to support.
2. **Lesson 2 — Explain a complex idea:** Choose a concept from study, technology or everyday life. Explain it using a person, problem and consequence.
3. **Lesson 3 — Evidence and story:** Pair an illustrative story with accurate facts. Make clear which details are examples and which are verified claims.
4. **Lesson 4 — Persuasive structure:** Build a short talk: problem, why it matters, evidence/example, proposed action and consequence.
5. **Lesson 5 — Delivery and critique:** Give a three-minute presentation. Ask whether the evidence supports the conclusion and whether the requested action is clear.

**Weekly assignment:** Create a three-to-five-minute presentation that explains or advocates for one idea. Include at least two factual supporting points and identify their sources.

**Deliverable:** `W16_persuasive_talk` and a one-page evidence sheet.

## Level 5 — Advanced narrative craft and capstone (Weeks 17–20)

### Week 17 — Nonlinear narratives and multiple perspectives

**Goal:** Control information and meaning through viewpoint and narrative order.

**Learn:** Multiple POV, alternating narrators, unreliable narrator, frame story, nested story, nonlinear chronology and parallel storylines.

**Resources:** OpenLearn Creative Writing and Critical Reading; Brandon Sanderson's writing lectures are optional for the fiction track.

1. **Lesson 1 — Multiple perspectives:** Retell one conflict from two characters' points of view. Give each a coherent, incomplete understanding.
2. **Lesson 2 — Unreliable narrator:** Write a narrator who misinterprets an event. Plant fair evidence that allows the audience to notice the gap.
3. **Lesson 3 — Nonlinear order:** Write a short event chronologically, then reorder scenes to create a purposeful question or reveal.
4. **Lesson 4 — Frame narrative:** Place a smaller story inside a larger one. Define why the outer narrator is telling it.
5. **Lesson 5 — Clarity check:** Ask a reader to map who knows what and when. Fix confusion that does not create intended mystery.

**Weekly assignment:** Write a 900–1,200 word story using two perspectives or a purposeful nonlinear structure. Include a short note explaining why you chose that structure.

**Deliverable:** `W17_non_linear_story`.

### Week 18 — Narrative psychology, culture and ethical storytelling

**Goal:** Understand audience expectations and emotional influence while respecting truth, context and agency.

**Learn:** Identification, expectation, empathy, cultural context, stereotype, representation, consent, manipulation and ethical framing.

**Resources:** The Moth true-story guidelines; TED-Ed Public Speaking 101; Acumen Storytelling for Change.

1. **Lesson 1 — Audience expectations:** Identify what an audience may assume based on genre, setting, character role or opening image.
2. **Lesson 2 — Empathy without simplification:** Give a character understandable motives without excusing harmful actions or flattening other people.
3. **Lesson 3 — Cultural specificity:** Use culturally specific detail accurately. Avoid making one character or community represent an entire group.
4. **Lesson 4 — Ethical true stories:** Check consent, privacy, power differences, accuracy and possible harm when telling stories involving real people.
5. **Lesson 5 — Persuasion audit:** Review a persuasive story. Identify any missing context, emotional pressure or unsupported claim.

**Weekly assignment:** Rewrite one earlier story to improve nuance, context or ethical handling. Add a short note describing what you changed and why.

**Deliverable:** `W18_ethics_revision`.

### Week 19 — Revision, adaptation and professional craft

**Goal:** Learn to improve a story through structural revision, line editing, feedback and adaptation.

**Learn:** Developmental editing, scene-level revision, line editing, proofreading, adaptation, script format, pitch and critique.

**Resources:** [OpenLearn: Creative Writing and Critical Reading](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/history-the-arts/creative-writing-and-critical-reading); [Brandon Sanderson's 2025 lecture notes](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/tagged/2025-lecture-notes) as optional fiction craft; Pixar feedback lesson.

1. **Lesson 1 — Structural revision:** Read an old draft and identify the real protagonist, goal, conflict, stakes and change. Fix structure before polishing sentences.
2. **Lesson 2 — Scene revision:** Remove repetitive scenes, strengthen cause and effect, and ensure each scene changes the situation.
3. **Lesson 3 — Line editing:** Improve clarity, rhythm, specific verbs, dialogue and unnecessary explanation. Preserve your natural voice.
4. **Lesson 4 — Adaptation:** Adapt one story into a 300-word written version, a 90-second spoken version and a six-frame storyboard. Preserve the central meaning, not every sentence.
5. **Lesson 5 — Feedback and pitch:** Ask a reviewer what they understood, where they lost interest and what felt most meaningful. Deliver a two-minute pitch and record feedback.

**Weekly assignment:** Produce Draft 2 of your strongest written story and a short adaptation plan.

**Deliverable:** `W19_final_story_draft_2` and `W19_revision_log`.

### Week 20 — Capstone, portfolio and final assessment

**Goal:** Demonstrate that you can create, adapt, perform and analyse stories across formats.

**Resources:** Revisit the strongest resources from the course; use the assessment rubric in Section 7.

1. **Lesson 1 — Select and plan:** Choose one original story or central idea. Write the premise, audience, intended effect and format plan.
2. **Lesson 2 — Create the written piece:** Produce a polished 1,000–1,500 word story or equivalent script. Focus on a clear character, conflict, stakes and change.
3. **Lesson 3 — Create the oral version:** Adapt the work into a three-to-five-minute performance. Practise from beats, not a memorised paragraph-by-paragraph script.
4. **Lesson 4 — Create the visual version:** Make a storyboard or a two-to-three-minute simple video. Use original, licensed or permission-cleared material.
5. **Lesson 5 — Present and review:** Share the written, spoken and visual pieces with a reviewer. Score yourself and write a final reflection identifying the next three skills to improve.

**Final assignment:** Submit the capstone portfolio described in Section 6.

**Deliverable:** `W20_storytelling_portfolio`.

# 6. Final portfolio: proof of skill

At the end of Week 20, your portfolio should contain these five projects.

## Project 1 — Original written story

- Length: approximately 1,000–1,500 words (or a complete short script of similar scope).
- Must include: a specific protagonist, goal, obstacle, stakes, turning point and meaningful resolution.
- Include: a one-paragraph premise, a beat outline and a revision note.
- Language: English or Hindi. A bilingual adaptation is an additional strength, not a requirement.

## Project 2 — Personal spoken story

- Length: 3–5 minutes.
- Must include: a hook, context, personal goal, conflict, turning point and reflection.
- Must be truthful. Do not present invented dialogue or uncertain memories as exact fact.
- Submit: cue-card outline, final recording and self-review.

## Project 3 — Visual story

- Length: 2–3 minutes, or a complete 10–15-frame storyboard if video production is not practical.
- Must communicate a clear change or event through image sequence, framing, action and sound.
- Submit: storyboard, final video or frame sequence, and a short explanation of creative choices.

## Project 4 — Explanatory or persuasive talk

- Length: 3–5 minutes.
- Must identify an audience, explain a meaningful problem or idea, use accurate supporting evidence and end with a clear takeaway or action.
- Submit: outline, source list and recording.

## Project 5 — Critical story analysis

Analyse three stories from different forms: one written/fictional story, one true spoken story and one film/video story.

For each, explain:

1. Who is the audience?
2. What does the central character or speaker want?
3. What is the conflict or central question?
4. How is information revealed?
5. What creates emotional or intellectual engagement?
6. What is the turning point or key decision?
7. What changes by the end?
8. What would you learn from this example?

# 7. Assessment system

Do not measure progress by the number of videos watched. Measure the quality of what you create and what an audience understands.

## A. Weekly scorecard — 100 points

Score the week's main assignment using this rubric. Use whole numbers from 1 to 5 for each category, then convert to points with the formula below.

| Category | Weight | What a strong result looks like |
|---|---:|---|
| Clarity | 15 | The audience understands what is happening and why it matters. |
| Structure | 15 | The story has a clear progression, turning point and ending. |
| Character / human focus | 15 | The central person feels specific and has a goal or meaningful perspective. |
| Conflict and stakes | 15 | There is a real obstacle, uncertainty or consequential question. |
| Emotional / intellectual impact | 15 | The story creates a response that feels earned rather than forced. |
| Originality and specificity | 10 | Details and perspective feel particular, not generic. |
| Language and delivery | 10 | The words, voice or visuals fit the audience and format. |
| Revision and craft | 5 | The work shows thoughtful improvement based on review. |
| **Total** | **100** | |

**Formula:** For each category, calculate `(rating ÷ 5) × category weight`, then add all category scores. Example: a rating of 4/5 for a 15-point category earns 12 points.

### Interpreting your score

| Score | Interpretation | Action |
|---:|---|---|
| 90–100 | Excellent control for your current level | Keep the work; identify one advanced experiment. |
| 80–89 | Strong | Revise the weakest category once. |
| 70–79 | Functional | Continue, but make the next week's practice target the weakest skill. |
| 60–69 | Developing | Revise the main assignment before moving to a harder version. |
| Below 60 | Not yet reliable | Relearn the concept, study an example and redo the exercise. |

These are self-study guide thresholds, not industry certification standards.

## B. External feedback questions

Ask at least one person to review important assignments. Do not ask only “Did you like it?” Ask:

1. What do you think the story was about?
2. Where did you become most interested?
3. Where did you lose interest or become confused?
4. What did you think the character wanted?
5. What changed by the end?
6. Which moment stayed with you afterward?
7. What is one thing you would cut or clarify?

A useful reviewer describes their experience. They do not need to be a professional writer.

## C. Monthly checkpoints

- **End of Week 4:** Can you create and explain a simple story outline? Can someone understand its goal and conflict?
- **End of Week 8:** Can you create a believable character and write a scene with a clear point of view and setting?
- **End of Week 12:** Can you create tension, emotional movement and a logical reveal?
- **End of Week 16:** Can you tell a true story aloud, create a short visual story and explain an idea persuasively?
- **End of Week 20:** Can you adapt one idea across writing, speech and visual formats and explain your choices?

## D. Revision rule

Every substantial project must have at least two versions:

- **Draft 1:** Get the idea onto the page or into the recording.
- **Draft 2:** Fix structure, clarity, pacing and audience understanding.

Do not polish every line before the story works as a whole. Structural problems are more important than perfect punctuation in an early draft.

# 10. Practical rules and common failure modes

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Starting with too much backstory:** Begin near the moment something changes. Add context when the audience needs it.
- **Confusing events with plot:** A sequence of things happening is not enough. Give events causes and consequences.
- **A passive protagonist:** The central character should make choices, even when events are outside their control.
- **Weak stakes:** “It matters” is not enough. Show what could be gained, lost or changed.
- **Explaining every emotion:** Let action, choice, detail and silence carry some meaning.
- **Twists without clues:** A surprise should change interpretation, not break the story's established rules.
- **Unnatural bilingual translation:** Adapt the line so it sounds like a real person in that language would say it.
- **Overusing formulas:** Frameworks are diagnostic tools, not laws. A story can use other structures if the audience can follow it and the choices serve the work.
- **Only watching videos:** Learning requires producing, reviewing and revising original work.
- **Only asking friends for praise:** Request specific feedback about clarity, interest, confusion and meaning.

## A simple quality-control checklist

Before you call a story finished, ask:

- Can I explain the premise in one or two sentences?
- Does the central character want something specific?
- Is there a real obstacle?
- Do the stakes matter to the character?
- Does each major scene change something?
- Is the ending connected to choices made earlier?
- Have I removed repetition and unnecessary explanation?
- Does the story fit its intended audience and format?
- If it is a true story, have I been honest about what I know and what I remember?
- If it involves another person, have I respected privacy and consent?
- If it uses factual claims, can I support them?

# 12. Start here: your first 60-minute lesson
